A phone that unfolds into a tablet is an impressive piece of engineering. A phone you can afford to keep working for six years is impressive in a more useful way.

Those achievements are too often treated as unrelated. We celebrate thinness, hinge tolerances, and larger displays, then treat the repair bill as somebody else's problem. When one damaged component makes keeping the whole device impractical, the engineering has transferred a cost to its owner.

Start with the owner looking at that repair bill. Most of the phone may still be useful: its processor, cameras, storage, and radios. One failed screen has made keeping all of them difficult to justify. When the whole device is retired over that screen, its remaining useful life goes with it.

Sometimes that obstacle is a costly part. Sometimes it is repair software, an activation server, or the only application that can properly edit years of work. And sometimes nothing has failed: the sales pitch has simply persuaded us that a perfectly adequate product is no longer enough. Those pressures meet at the same ownership question: **can I keep this useful, and feel satisfied doing so?**

Folding phones are a revealing place to start because the failure is tangible. Following the repair beyond the screen leads to the less visible dependencies that govern tractors, cars, games, and creative software. Repair rights can make those dependencies easier to maintain. Reverse engineering, increasingly assisted by AI, can sometimes help owners work around the ones left closed.

## A Folding Phone Should Be Designed for Its Second Repair

iFixit's August 2026 [Galaxy Z Fold8 teardown](https://www.ifixit.com/News/118647/galaxy-z-fold8-teardown-samsungs-best-foldable-still-has-a-hinge-problem) shows why repairability deserves attention alongside durability. Reaching one battery requires removing the outer display. The inner display did not survive the team's disassembly. Its fine-powder test also interfered with hinge operation, although iFixit explicitly says that experiment was not an IP certification test. These observations reveal repair obstacles; they do not establish how often owners' phones fail.

Samsung does offer repairs. Its [US pricing page](https://www.samsung.com/us/support/cracked-screen-repair/) shows how expensive keeping a folding display working can be, including on older models:

| Phone | Inner-screen repair | Inner-screen module replacement |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Galaxy Z Fold8 | $419 | $579 |
| Galaxy Z Fold5 | $429 | $539 |
| Galaxy Z Fold4 | $444 | $559 |

These are suggested US prices checked on October 20, 2026, before taxes and fees. Screen-only eligibility excludes certain other damage; a module replacement includes additional components. Prices and availability vary. The cheaper column is not available for every damaged phone.

The Fold5 and Fold4 make the aging problem concrete: a phone can be several generations old and still carry a screen bill above $400. Its processor may remain fast enough and its cameras perfectly adequate. Its resale value can fall while the repair price stays high.

Consider a hypothetical older phone whose working equivalent costs $350 used. A $429 screen repair would cost more than replacing it with that equivalent. That is an illustration, not a current resale quote. The original flagship price is money already spent; the owner must compare today's repair bill with today's alternatives, likely remaining life, and downtime. A device can have years of useful computing left and still lose that calculation.

Apple's [new iPhone Duo](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/09/apple-unveils-iphone-duo/) brings the same ownership question to the iPhone. Announced in September, it is scheduled to reach customers on October 23, 2026. Its folding display and precision hinge may be impressive; years of affordable service remain something it has to prove. The question for a buyer is what replacing that inner screen will mean in year three or four, when the launch excitement and much of the resale value have gone.

This is how an expensive product can become economically disposable without being literally unrepairable. The criticism does not require proving that every foldable fails early, or that its designers wanted it to fail. It asks whether the design makes ordinary failures affordable to recover from.

## Good Enough Should Be Enough

If the 4K television you bought six years ago still produces a picture you enjoy, a new model does not make that picture worse. If your Toyota is old enough to vote and still gets you where you need to go safely and reliably, its age alone is a poor reason to replace it. The same goes for a phone that takes the photos you want and runs the apps you use. A product can finish paying for itself and keep being useful.

New products can bring worthwhile improvements. A brighter screen, an accessibility feature, or a capability you actually need can justify a change. But an improvement on a specification sheet does not create an obligation to buy it. The useful comparison is with your own needs, including whatever has changed since the last purchase.

Consumerism works on that sense of sufficiency. The recurring message is that satisfaction should expire: this year's model makes last year's look tired; a new feature becomes something you are missing; a working possession becomes credit toward its replacement. There is always another launch coming. You can appreciate the engineering and still decide that what you have is enough.

## Replacement Is Sold Before Anything Breaks

Upgrade programs give that message a payment schedule. Samsung's [New Galaxy Club in the UK](https://www.samsung.com/uk/new-galaxy-club/) promotes upgrading from month 12, with a guaranteed 50% trade-in value during months 12 through 15, subject to its terms. Samsung's [US S25 version](https://www.samsung.com/us/smartphones/new-galaxy-club/) similarly offered an upgrade benefit after 12 monthly program payments. Its page now limits enrollment to selected S25 purchases before June 20, 2025, so that particular offer should be understood as a past program, not an invitation to enroll today.

Apple and carriers sell their own versions of that rhythm. [Apple Upgrade](https://www.apple.com/shop/apple-upgrade) offers 12- or 24-month iPhone leases, with the current device returned when upgrading. [AT&T Next Up Anytime](https://www.att.com/plans/next-up-anytime/) charges an extra $10 a month for earlier upgrade eligibility, with qualifying turn-in and a limit of three upgrades per 12 months. The payment buys an upgrade option; it is an additional cost to compare with simply keeping the phone.

These programs make the next purchase a scheduled event. The sales question becomes whether you are eligible to upgrade, even if your current phone still does everything you need. On Samsung's own repair page, the invitation is especially direct: **"Skip the repairs"** and upgrade to a new or refurbished device.

Trade-in does not automatically mean disposal. A returned phone can serve another owner, and buying refurbished can keep useful hardware in circulation. That deserves credit. The pressure I object to is the assumption that a working phone should leave your pocket on a sales calendar. A secondhand market and longer first-owner use can both be valuable; constant replacement is not a prerequisite for either.

Blaming consumers for replacing things misses how the choice is structured. A new product has a visible price, a warranty, and a delivery date. Repair can involve a diagnostic charge, an uncertain parts supply, and a week without the device needed for work. When manufacturers make replacement predictable and repair uncertain, people respond to those incentives.

The waste is measurable. The ITU and UNITAR's [Global E-waste Monitor 2024](https://ewastemonitor.info/the-global-e-waste-monitor-2024/) reports **62 million tonnes of e-waste in 2022**, up 82% from 2010. Only 22.3% was documented as formally collected and recycled. Those figures cover a broad waste stream; they do not tell us how much was caused by foldables, repair restrictions, or people discarding working equipment.

They do put the replacement habit in perspective. We are building a growing stock of electronics and a growing waste stream alongside it. Repair has to compete with the convenience of buying again.

The FTC's [Nixing the Fix report](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2021/05/ftc-report-congress-examines-anti-competitive-repair-restrictions-recommends-ways-expand-consumers) identified adhesives, restricted parts, and unavailable diagnostic software as barriers. It found little evidence supporting manufacturers' broad justifications for repair restrictions. That finding deserves more weight than the assumption that every obstacle exists for the customer's safety.

The cultural change I want is simple: keeping a useful device running should count as competence. A repaired phone, an old laptop, or a television that has survived several product launches should not need an apology. Choosing to keep one is a decision you get to make. But even an owner who ignores every advertisement can struggle to keep a device running when a needed part or service becomes unavailable.

## The Tractor Shows What a Software Lock Can Cost

The folding phone begins with the price and difficulty of replacing a part. A tractor adds another obstacle: even after the part is fitted, software can determine whether the repair is complete. A farmer may still need access to a tool that clears a fault or pairs the replacement component.

The FTC's January 2025 case against Deere alleged that restricted repair software forced owners toward authorized dealers, raising costs and delaying service. In July 2026, the FTC and five states [announced a settlement with a proposed stipulated order](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/07/ftc-states-secure-settlement-deere-company-advancing-farmers-right-repair). Its terms would require equivalent repair resources for owners and independent shops on fair and reasonable terms, including component pairing and fault-code operations. The announcement expressly notes that an order becomes binding when approved and signed by the judge.

The dispute illustrates how software can decide who gets to complete work on hardware. A socket wrench cannot supply a missing authorization capability.

Repair rights should make diagnosis and restoration available without granting a license to defeat emissions rules or safety systems. A lawful repair and a prohibited modification remain different acts. Manufacturers should have to justify the restriction on the particular operation, rather than using the existence of sensitive functions to close the entire toolbox.

## PlayStation and Kindle Make Dependence Personal

That distinction between fitting a part and being allowed to use it reaches into the living room. It also changes what repairability means: a removable component is only part of the answer if getting it working requires a service the owner cannot maintain.

Sony's [detachable PS5 disc-drive documentation](https://direct.playstation.com/en-us/buy-accessories/disc-drive-for-ps5-digital-edition-consoles) requires an internet connection to pair the drive with the console during setup. The modular drive is a useful physical design choice. Its activation still depends on an external service.

That is a setup requirement, not evidence that every disc needs a fresh online check. It nevertheless creates a long-term question: what happens when someone needs to replace that drive after the pairing service is gone? An offline recovery route would make the answer part of the product rather than a future promise.

Kindle demonstrates a service withdrawal that has already happened. Amazon ended specified services on affected Kindle models released in 2012 or earlier on May 20, 2026. The [staff response on Amazon's support forum](https://www.amazonforum.com/s/question/0D5at00000iWVhICAW/support-of-older-kindle) distinguishes the lost ability to obtain books through Amazon from continued reading of downloaded books. The hardware retains useful functions, with fewer ways to use them.

The folding phone and the old Kindle arrive at the problem from opposite directions. One needs a physical repair; the other can retain a functioning screen and battery while losing an important route for receiving books. Ending an old service may be reasonable. Local files, documented formats, and a viable offline mode determine how much useful life survives that decision.

Kindle owners have also built ways to regain control. The community maintains jailbreaks including WinterBreak, AdBreak, and [SpiderCat](https://github.com/KindleModding/SpiderCat), with availability depending on the device and firmware. Unlocking a supported reader lets its owner install software such as [KOReader](https://github.com/koreader/koreader/wiki/Installation-on-Kindle-devices), which can read locally stored EPUBs and other supported formats. A good e-ink screen can keep earning its place with a library the owner manages.

There is a distinction between unlocking the reader and preserving every purchased book. KOReader does not support DRM-protected books, and a jailbreak does not automatically restore Amazon services or make an entire account library portable. The practical gain is greater control over the device and compatible local files. Shared code, documentation, and years of volunteer maintenance make that gain usable.

## Electric Cars Need a Life Beyond Their Manufacturer

Move that dependence from a reader to a car and the cost of losing support becomes much harder to absorb. Tesla offers [public repair resources](https://www.tesla.com/support/service-portal), free on-vehicle Service Mode, and paid [Toolbox and Service Mode Plus](https://service.tesla.com/en-US/diagnostic-software) for more advanced work. Some security-related actions require additional verification. Independent access exists, with conditions that matter to repair businesses.

Fisker owners face a different problem: maintaining vehicles after the manufacturer's collapse. The community's [Fiskeretta Diagnostics project](https://github.com/Fiskeretta/fiskeretta-diagnostics) describes an open tool for reading vehicle information and diagnostic faults, built on protocol knowledge shared by other owners. That is a concrete form of regained capability, not proof that a community tool replaces every factory repair function.

A vehicle's support plan should account for the business disappearing. Manuals, parts, diagnostic interfaces, and a way to maintain essential software all belong in that plan. Battery packs and safety systems also need qualified procedures; an AI-generated explanation is not a validation of a repair.

The same continuity problem exists in connected appliances. An oven or washing machine should retain its essential local functions when an app, account, or cloud service ends. Internet access should add useful features without becoming a second power switch controlled elsewhere.

## Communities Give Hardware Another Life

The Xbox 360 makes that persistence especially tangible. A console launched in 2005 is still getting new possibilities in 2026. Grimdoomer's [Peer Pressure](https://github.com/grimdoomer/Xbox360PeerPressure) is a persistent software mod that lets supported consoles run homebrew applications and boot alternative software. Its [initial public release on October 7](https://github.com/grimdoomer/Xbox360PeerPressure/releases/tag/v1.0.477) offers a software installation route without soldering, and the modification survives restarts. That lowers the barrier compared with fitting a hardware mod.

Easier still has limits: Peer Pressure needs a compatible hard drive, excludes Corona and Winchester console revisions, and changes internal storage in ways that can leave a console unable to boot if something goes wrong. It is not a universal, risk-free button. But it is a striking answer to the assumption that old hardware has nothing left to offer. People are still finding ways to make a twenty-year-old platform more useful.

On the PS5, the recent [Byepervisor Reborn](https://github.com/PS5Dev/Byepervisor-Reborn) exploit opens another path for research into the console's protected system software. Its maintainers describe it as a foundation for developers, requiring other exploit components, rather than a finished consumer jailbreak. They credit an earlier discovery by TheFloW and later rediscoveries with assistance from large language models. Human security research and AI tools are meeting in work that can expand what owners are able to investigate and change.

That community work connects directly to the folding phone's repair bill. In both cases, the owner wants to preserve value that is already there. A replacement screen restores access to functioning hardware; community software can recover uses that a closed platform prevents. Neither should require buying the whole product again.

## AI Can Lower the Cost of Understanding a Product

Keeping hardware useful requires replacement parts; keeping software-dependent products useful can also require recovering undocumented behavior. A new display cannot restore a missing server, and an AI agent cannot manufacture an affordable display. Each addresses a different obstacle to continued use.

Reverse engineering long predates generative AI. Researchers have spent decades tracing binaries, documenting file formats, and rebuilding behavior. Today's useful change is that coding agents can help navigate those tools and turn observations into candidate implementations and tests.

[AnyPS5](https://github.com/boykopovar/AnyPS5) shows that work reaching modern games. The project converts PS5 executables into native Windows or Linux executables and supplies replacement libraries for console services, along with graphics translation. The game runs locally on the PC; this is not Remote Play streaming from a PS5. Contributors have reported gameplay in titles including [Dreaming Sarah](https://github.com/boykopovar/AnyPS5/discussions/3003) and [Animal Well](https://github.com/boykopovar/AnyPS5/discussions/966), with specific builds, hardware, and test results.

Those reports are real progress, but they do not make the whole PS5 library playable on a PC. Compatibility remains experimental, and reaching an opening scene does not establish that a game works from beginning to end. Contributors [disclose using AI for coding, debugging, testing, and analysis](https://github.com/boykopovar/AnyPS5/discussions/675); the project's [contribution rules](https://github.com/boykopovar/AnyPS5/blob/main/CONTRIBUTING.md) keep responsibility with the people submitting the work. AI can help more people tackle the missing pieces. The community still has to establish what actually works.

[Reverse Engineer Anything](https://github.com/morluto/rea), for example, connects agents to tools such as Ghidra, Hopper, and IDA. Its documented outputs include pseudocode, assembly, strings, and relationships between routines. An investigator uses those clues to reconstruct behavior; recovering the original source tree is a different claim.

REA's [DX-Ball case study](https://rea.tools/showcase/dx-ball/) supplies a small, concrete example. At its October 7 checkpoint, the maintainers report an agent-assisted reconstruction of a sound-panning function, checked against the original x86 code in 3,205 cases and matching all 63 compiled function bytes. Work on a playable reconstruction was still continuing. One verified routine is useful progress, and the published checks make the claim more informative than a video of a game apparently running.

For an owner, a modest result can be valuable: an importer that retrieves old project files, documentation for a device protocol, or a local replacement for one abandoned service. Reproducing the entire commercial product is often unnecessary.

Long before the current AI tools, game-decompilation communities established demanding ways to check their work. The [Perfect Dark matching-decompilation project](https://github.com/n64decomp/perfect_dark) describes source that builds with the original compiler into byte-identical output. It requires an existing game ROM and extracts assets from it. That human-led work supplies a verification standard for AI-assisted projects.

A modern port is a further engineering task. Reconstructing code, reproducing behavior on another platform, and distributing the game's artwork and audio involve different work and different rights. A playable opening scene does not establish correct timing, saved-game behavior, or completion of the game.

The same caution applies to AI output. A [September 2026 research preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05370) found that some LLM decompilations which compiled and passed the supplied tests still behaved differently on other legitimate inputs. Its findings concern the systems and test sets studied, but the lesson is practical: fluent code and a green test suite can conceal a wrong reconstruction.

AI can make investigation more accessible. Durable ownership still depends on people who check the results, explain the remaining uncertainty, and maintain the software after the demonstration.

## Photoshop and ArtCraft Put File Ownership to the Test

For creative software, the thing we want to keep useful may be years of work rather than a physical device. [Adobe's cancellation guidance](https://helpx.adobe.com/account/individual/subscriptions-and-plans/renewals-and-cancellations/account-access-after-plan-cancellation.html) says paid application access ends after the billing period, while work saved on the computer remains accessible. Keeping a PSD on disk does not by itself preserve the workflow used to edit it.

A subscription does not promise perpetual access to the application. The ownership question is whether users have a practical way to preserve and move the work they created with it.

ArtCraft's [PhotoCraft](https://github.com/storytold/photocraft) offers an interesting response. The project describes itself as a clean-room Photoshop reimplementation in Rust, with layered PSD support and an open-source codebase. Its own README also calls it early alpha and says it is not yet a replacement for daily professional Photoshop work. Typography, tools, and plug-in compatibility are among the gaps. The clean-room description is the project's claim, not an independent legal determination.

That makes it a promising compatibility effort with substantial work ahead. A replacement earns trust by opening a real document, letting someone edit and save it, and preserving the details their work requires.

This is where AI-assisted development could change the economics of exit. A smaller team may be able to implement enough of a format or workflow to give users another choice. Source availability then lets others inspect and continue that work. The benefit depends on file fidelity, reliable builds, and maintenance, not the number of familiar menu entries.

## Europe Has Already Changed the Hardware Rules

Owners should not have to reconstruct a product to keep using it. That brings the argument back to the phone: its repair options, battery, and charging port are choices made before it reaches the shop. Europe deserves credit for turning some of those choices into obligations.

The EU's [common-charger rules](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/eu-common-charger-rules-power-all-your-devices-single-charger-2024-12-28_en) require USB-C on covered wired-charging phones placed on its market from December 28, 2024, with laptops following on April 28, 2026. Apple introduced USB-C with the [iPhone 15 in September 2023](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2023/09/apple-debuts-iphone-15-and-iphone-15-plus/), ahead of the deadline. The law made continued reliance on a proprietary charging port untenable for covered EU sales. That is a concrete gain for people who want their cables and chargers to outlive one brand's product cycle.

The EU's [phone and tablet ecodesign rules](https://energy-efficient-products.ec.europa.eu/product-list/smartphones-and-tablets_en), applying since June 20, 2025, go further into the product: battery endurance, spare parts, repair information, and access to software needed for repair. These are obligations that can shape engineering decisions before a device reaches the customer.

The push for replaceable batteries matters just as much, but the dates and exceptions matter too. The general portable-battery replacement provisions begin applying on February 18, 2027. The Commission's [July 2026 guidance](https://environment.ec.europa.eu/document/download/17491562-50cc-443a-897f-b50848ab0a8a_en?filename=C_2026_5032_1_EN_ACT_part1_v13.pdf) explains that smartphone-specific ecodesign provisions take precedence. Those allow a route involving stronger durability requirements and professional replacement, alongside a route for replacement by an ordinary user. This is progress toward replaceable batteries, with limits; it does not promise that every 2027 phone will have a back you can pop off by hand.

The EU's separate [right-to-repair framework](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/policies/right-to-repair-products/) set a July 31, 2026 deadline for national transposition, with obligations covering specified products. Europe has supplied a useful model: write continued service and compatibility into the rules of the market, then make manufacturers design around them.

## America Is Debating What Buying a Game Should Mean

The parallel demand for games is continuity after the seller stops providing support. A replaceable battery helps a phone survive a worn-out cell; a planned offline mode could help a game survive the loss of its server.

In the United States, Representative Ritchie Torres [announced on October 15, 2026](https://ritchietorres.house.gov/posts/rep-torres-announces-power-to-the-players-act-legislation-to-ensure-gamers-own-their-games) that he would introduce the **Power To The Players Act**. His office describes a proposal directing the FTC to protect game ownership, continued play, and transfer, including an offline mode before supporting servers shut down. As of this article's October 20 check, the cited announcement describes intended legislation, not an enacted right. The eventual text and enforcement rules will determine what it can deliver.

And yes, ["Power to the Players" is also GameStop's slogan](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2019/03/27/1773986/0/en/GameStop-Makes-a-Play-with-Multiple-Esports-Partners-Supporting-Amateur-Players-Nationwide.html). A retail tagline finding a second career in Congress is funny. Whether it becomes an enforceable right to keep playing is the serious part.

An offline requirement also needs careful design. A game built around a shared server cannot always be preserved by removing a login screen. A useful law must specify what functionality survives, what publishers must prepare before shutdown, and how customers can enforce the promise. I want that engineering work considered while a game is being built, instead of left to volunteers after its owner has lost access.

## Ownership Needs Both Tools and Rights

Better tools cannot settle the legal questions by themselves. In the United States, [DMCA section 1201](https://www.copyright.gov/1201/2024/) regulates bypassing access controls separately from ordinary copyright infringement, with limited exemptions. The [2024 final rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-10-28/pdf/2024-24563.pdf) includes defined repair and preservation uses; it does not make every bypass or public game port lawful. Owning hardware, holding a software license, and having permission to redistribute content are distinct.

My test for a product is whether its owner has a workable route through four problems:

- **A part wears out:** replacement parts and instructions remain obtainable at a reasonable cost.
- **Software rejects the repair:** diagnostics and necessary calibration or pairing are available to the owner or a chosen repairer.
- **The vendor leaves:** essential local functions continue, with a documented end-of-support route.
- **The owner wants to leave:** data can move into another tool without losing the work that gives it value.

Security belongs in that design. So do compensation for creators and the real cost of maintaining software. None requires treating every owner as an adversary or every independent repair as a threat.

Resisting the replacement cycle is getting harder and easier in different ways. Hardware that already meets your needs gives you a reason to wait. Repair communities, used parts, open software, and better reverse-engineering tools can give you ways to keep it working. At the same time, restricted repairs and dependence on accounts or servers can take that choice away. Feeling content with a device will not restore a service that has shut down. That is why personal restraint and repair rights belong in the same argument.

Return to the folding phone with the damaged screen. The owner needs an affordable part, a repair they can complete, and software that will keep supporting the repaired device. Solving only one of those problems can still leave a useful phone headed for replacement.

The tractor owner, the player, and the designer need different remedies for the same practical reason: the value they paid for has not disappeared just because one dependency has become expensive or unavailable. AI may help recover some missing capabilities. Good product design and enforceable rights can make that recovery unnecessary.

There will always be another attempt to sell you something new. You do not have to accept the suggestion that everything you already own has become inadequate. Keeping useful things working should be an ordinary part of ownership, and being satisfied with them should be allowed to last.
